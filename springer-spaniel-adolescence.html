English Springer Spaniel Adolescence: When the Teenage Phase Starts, How Long It Lasts and What to Do When Training Falls Apart

Rebuild everyday skills and measure progress at your own dog’s pace.

By Grace Pemberton · English Springer Spaniel Guide · October 15, 2026

If English Springer Spaniel adolescence has turned a reliable puppy into a distracted training partner, start by changing the difficulty of the task. Your neighbor’s polished dog is not a useful deadline for yours. Keep the relationship steady and measure what your own Springer can do in a particular setting.

Quick answer: Plan for a teenage period beginning around six months and potentially continuing toward two years, using the general canine window described below rather than a Springer-specific countdown. Rebuild familiar skills in easier places, reward successful responses, and manage outdoor freedom while recall is unreliable. Maturity does not replace practice.

When does the Springer teenage phase start?

Around six months is a useful planning point, not a guaranteed onset date. Dogs Trust describes its adolescent training period as six to 24 months; that is a general dog age bracket, not research establishing an English Springer Spaniel timetable.

Use the calendar to prepare, then watch the task

Write down where a familiar cue still works and where it has become unreliable. “Turns toward me in the kitchen but keeps sniffing beside the park gate” gives you a starting point. “Has become impossible” does not tell you what to change. Keep the description neutral enough that another family member could recognize the same event.

Before increasing freedom, arrange places where you can practice without relying on a perfect response. Prepare rewards before opening the door, agree on the cue words, and choose a quieter route when necessary. You do not need to wait for a dramatic teenage moment to make these arrangements. They are useful whenever your current training plan exceeds your dog’s current skill.

How long does English Springer Spaniel adolescence last?

The broad planning window can extend toward two years, but there is no supported birthday here when every Springer becomes dependable. Separate developmental age from the skill you are trying to teach. A dog can become older while still needing practice around birds, visitors, or an exciting gateway.

Look for repeatable improvements

Choose a modest goal such as turning toward you on a quiet path when you say the dog’s name. Keep the location, reward, and distraction level reasonably consistent while assessing it. Then change one feature: move to a different quiet path, or practice farther from home. Avoid making every session harder simply because yesterday went well.

Progress might mean choosing a quieter entrance without a struggle, returning from an ordinary sniff, or completing a short practice session with both of you comfortable. These are examples of personal goals, not age milestones. Keep them in your notes even when someone else’s dog can already perform a spectacular retrieve. Their result does not invalidate your useful, repeatable improvement.

Why does training seem to fall apart?

Adolescent changes in responsiveness have research support, but that does not mean your Springer has forgotten everything or is deliberately challenging your authority. In Newcastle University’s report of canine adolescence research, dogs were less responsive to their caregiver’s sit cue at eight months than at five months. The tested obedience group involved retrievers and their crosses, so those ages cannot define a Springer-specific peak.

Distinguish the cue from the setting

Ask whether the behavior works somewhere easier. If it does, use that setting as your practice base. If it does not, rebuild the behavior there before moving outdoors. This is a practical way to organize training, not a home experiment that proves why the behavior changed.

For a walking exercise, that might mean rewarding a voluntary step beside you in the driveway instead of requiring a long stretch beside a busy field. For attention, start with a nearby response in a quiet room instead of calling across the yard during a visitor’s arrival. Keep your cue clear and your next request achievable. You are teaching a behavior in context, not collecting evidence that the dog is being difficult.

What does the Springer’s background change?

Plan for an active dog that needs ongoing guidance. The AKC’s English Springer Spaniel profile describes a bird dog with considerable energy and stamina. Use that background when choosing purposeful activities; the description does not supply a universal daily exercise prescription.

Give exploration a place in the plan

Try an ordinary sniffing opportunity between easy training moments rather than demanding continuous attention throughout the walk. Choose where exploration can happen and where you need the dog close. At a narrow entrance, prioritize getting through calmly; in a suitable open area, allow room to investigate while keeping control of the outing.

Do not turn a breed description into a comparison contest between working dogs, show dogs, littermates, or social media accounts. Ask the breeder or trainer about your particular dog’s experience, then build goals around what you observe. “More energetic than the dog in that video” is not a training plan. “Practice returning from a low-interest sniff in this enclosed area” is.

How do you rebuild recall without gambling on it?

Return to an easier, controlled location and make coming back worthwhile. Dogs Trust’s recall guidance recommends beginning in enclosed settings, increasing distance and distractions gradually, and using a long line attached to a harness outdoors. It also advises staying calm after a missed response and rewarding returns.

Make the next repetition easier

Prepare a reward the dog is interested in, get their attention nearby, give the household’s agreed recall cue, and welcome them back. Deliver the reward where you want the return to finish. Sometimes release the dog to explore again afterward, when appropriate, so your practice does not consist entirely of ending interesting activities.

If the dog does not respond, stop escalating the request. Use your management arrangement to prevent wandering, move to an easier position, and reassess the setup. Do not jerk the line or turn the return into a confrontation. If handling the line and rewards together is difficult, practice the equipment in a quiet enclosed place with a trainer before adding outdoor distractions.

Emily Larlham’s Kikopup demonstration shows the initial recall lesson and conditioning the cue; use it to visualize the easy starting stage, not as proof of outdoor reliability.

A practical reset you can follow step by step

Use this as an adjustable practice sequence. The sample counts and examples are organizational choices, not a promise that a particular dose of training will resolve adolescence.

  1. Choose one behavior. Write an observable goal: “comes toward me when called in the enclosed yard.” Avoid combining recall, greeting manners, leash walking, and settling into the same test.
  2. Choose the easiest useful setting. Start where you can arrange success without an exciting distraction arriving unexpectedly. Check the gate and prepare the reward before beginning.
  3. Record the baseline. Note a small set of ordinary opportunities, including whether the first cue worked and whether you helped. Do not quietly count repeated calls as an immediate response.
  4. Practice the easy version. Ask while the dog can attend, reward the response, and stop while you can still make clear decisions. A brief successful session is a reasonable goal.
  5. Change one demand. Add a little distance, a new quiet location, or a mild distraction. Keep the other demands easy. Return to the previous version if the new setup repeatedly fails.
  6. Review your own notes. Compare equivalent settings, choose the next manageable step, and tell everyone in the household what you are practicing. Keep outdoor management until the actual setting supports more freedom.

Common mistakes that make progress harder to judge

Avoid changing the task and the scoring system together. If you move from a quiet yard to a crowded field, the results are not a fair comparison of the same exercise.

Repeating cues and hiding help

Record what actually happened: a first-cue return, a return after repeated calls, or a return after you moved closer. All are observations, but they are different observations. Honest notes help you choose the next setting. They do not need to make the dog look impressive.

Replacing every quiet activity with more excitement

Build an outing around a manageable mix of exploration, easy practice, and time afterward without demands. Avoid adding a harder game whenever you feel training went badly. Decide what the activity is for before starting it, and retain ordinary household routines. Include low-pressure handling practice in that routine; the Springer grooming schedule can help organize recurring care tasks without turning every interaction into an obedience test.

Calling every difficulty “teenage behavior”

Keep notes about the situation rather than assuming every problem has the same explanation. This article stays with everyday skill practice.

How should you measure your own dog’s progress?

Measure a specific response in a specific setting, then compare it with your earlier notes from that setting. Leave other dogs out of the score. A useful record ends with a practical decision rather than a label about your Springer’s character.

A sample entry you can adapt

Write: “Quiet enclosed yard, recall, came toward me after the first cue, reward delivered nearby; next time keep this setup.” After a harder session, write: “Near the open park entrance, continued looking outward; next time use the enclosed yard.” These are illustrative notes, not reports from a real dog or predicted outcomes.

Keep a separate line for your own workload: whether you had rewards ready, chose a workable route, and stopped before getting frustrated. Those are actions you control. If repeated simplification produces no useful progress, take the notes to a qualified reward-based trainer and ask them to watch the setup. Ask for a demonstration you can repeat at home and a clear way to decide when the next step is appropriate.

Frequently asked questions

At what age do Springers become teenagers?

Around six months is a practical preparation point within the general canine adolescent window. It is not a proven Springer-specific onset date; watch changes in your own dog’s responsiveness and context.

Will my Springer automatically calm down at two years?

Do not use that birthday as a guarantee. The general age bracket is a planning guide, while everyday manners still need practice and management suited to the situation.

Has my adolescent dog forgotten recall?

A missed return does not establish that. Check whether the behavior still works in an easier enclosed setting and rebuild there before increasing distance or distractions.

Should I stop rewarding cues my dog already knows?

Keep useful rewards in the rebuilding plan. Make responding worthwhile while you practice familiar skills in manageable settings, especially when outdoor distractions make the task harder.

How do I know when to ask a trainer for help?

Ask when you cannot arrange manageable practice, cannot comfortably handle the equipment, or see no useful progress after simplifying the task. Bring specific observations so the trainer can help you adjust the setup.

Your next manageable step

English Springer Spaniel adolescence calls for a flexible plan: use the broad age window as context, rebuild an achievable version of the skill, and protect your practice with sensible management. Pick one behavior and one place for the next session. Compare the result with your own starting point, and let that evidence choose what comes next.
